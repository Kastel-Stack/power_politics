# Axes of Power

*A practical model for building influence, resources, networks, systems and opportunity*

**Mark Ashton Smith**  
**20 June 2026**

Power is often described too narrowly.

It is often reduced to domination, status, persuasion, wealth, hierarchy or the ability to make other people comply. But that is only one side of power, and often the most pathological side.

A fuller model begins somewhere else.

**Power is the capacity to shape the conditions under which people perceive, decide, coordinate and act.**

In healthy form, power expands the field of possible action. It creates more viable roles, more reachable futures, better public models, richer feedback, more adaptive experiments and more flexible norms.

In pathological form, power narrows the field. It captures attention, freezes roles, suppresses feedback, hardens hierarchy, manufactures false public models and protects brittle legitimacy.

This distinction matters because modern power is becoming increasingly system-like. Nation states, platforms, financial institutions, AI companies and multinational corporations increasingly shape not only what people can do, but what they can see, say, imagine, test and coordinate around. The deepest struggle is no longer simply over resources. It is over the generative conditions of reality: attention, models, narratives, norms, infrastructure, data and institutional legitimacy.

That is the frame for Kastel Futures.

The central question is not merely:

> Who has power?

It is:

> Does this form of power expand or narrow the system’s capacity for reality-coupled exploration, constraint, coordination and consolidation?

Healthy power increases adaptive capacity. Pathological power captures the field.

The eight axes below offer a practical model for understanding power not as a single trait, tactic or possession, but as a stack of capacities. Together, they describe how individuals, organisations and communities become capable of shaping reality without losing contact with it.

## Figure 1. The eight-axis model

The eight-axis model treats power as a stack of capacities rather than a single possession.

| Axis | Core question | Power function |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Self-positioning** | Do I appear credible, agentic and high-status? | Shapes whether others can imagine you as a consequential actor. |
| **2. Resource control** | Do I control something others need? | Shapes dependency, leverage and access to future options. |
| **3. Network architecture** | Am I positioned in the right social graph? | Shapes information flow, sponsorship, coalition power and opportunity. |
| **4. Agenda and framing control** | Can I define the game before others debate it? | Shapes public models, decision criteria and what becomes thinkable. |
| **5. Influence and exchange** | Can I move people without brute force? | Shapes trust, reciprocity, identification and voluntary alignment. |
| **6. Power deployment** | Do I actually use power to produce outcomes? | Converts stored capacity into visible results and legitimacy. |
| **7. Organisational/system capacity** | Can I turn influence into repeatable machinery? | Converts personal power into systems, memory, routines and resilience. |
| **8. Opportunity/breakthrough capacity** | Can I detect and act on rare, high-upside openings? | Captures convex opportunities before consensus forms. |

## Figure 2. The IQ/EQ kernel

The IQ/EQ kernel sits underneath all eight axes.

| Kernel capacity | Function |
|---|---|
| **IQ** | Models systems, risks, opportunities and causal structure. |
| **EQ** | Reads people, regulates emotion and builds trust. |

Healthy power expands the field and supports adaptive evolution and sustainable growth.

Pathological power does the reverse. It narrows the field, captures attention, suppresses feedback, freezes hierarchy and protects false models.

So the question for Kastel Futures is not only:

> How do we gain power?

It is:

> What kind of power are we building, and what kind of future does it make possible?

That is the line between empowerment and capture.

Healthy power expands agency.

Pathological power encloses it.

The task now is to build systems, institutions, businesses, tools and personal capacities that expand the generative field rather than capture it.

# 1. Self-positioning

The first axis is self-positioning.

This is the power of appearing like someone who belongs near consequential decisions.

It includes presence, confidence, dress, voice, timing, symbolic cues, reputation and personal brand. It also includes the ability to stop disqualifying yourself before anyone else has done so.

Many people lose power before the game begins because they act as though they are waiting to be selected. They over-explain, under-claim, defer too quickly, avoid visibility, or treat ambition as something slightly embarrassing.

Self-positioning does not mean pretending to be more competent than you are. It means making your competence visible enough to matter.

The core question is:

> Do I appear credible, agentic and high-status enough to be taken seriously?

Power begins when others can imagine you as a consequential actor.

# 2. Resource control

The second axis is resource control.

Power increases when you control something others need.

That resource might be money, expertise, infrastructure, information, access, data, technology, distribution, credibility, social connection, or the ability to solve a problem that others cannot solve.

This is where dependency matters. If you depend entirely on someone else, your bargaining position is weak. If they depend on you, your position strengthens. If both sides depend on each other, power becomes relational rather than one-sided.

The practical rule is simple:

> Reduce your own dependency while increasing the value of what you can provide.

This is why expertise matters. This is why ownership matters. This is why access matters. This is why scarce skills matter.

Status without resources is fragile. Resources without visibility may be underused. But together, they create leverage.

# 3. Network architecture

The third axis is network architecture.

Power is not located only inside the individual. It is distributed through the social graph.

Who knows you? Who trusts you? Who will speak for you when you are not in the room? Which groups do you connect? Which coalitions can you activate? Which people give you information before it becomes public?

A weak network gives you friends. A strong network gives you reach, protection, intelligence and opportunity.

This is why sponsors are different from mentors. A mentor advises you. A sponsor spends political capital on your behalf. They recommend you, defend you, promote you, and open doors when you are absent.

Brokerage is especially powerful. If you sit between groups that do not normally communicate, you gain informational advantage. You can translate between worlds. You can see opportunities before insiders in either group see them.

The core question is:

> Am I positioned inside the right flows of information, trust and opportunity?

Power grows when you become a node other people need to pass through.

# 4. Agenda and framing control

The fourth axis is agenda and framing control.

This is the power to shape the game before others start playing it.

Many people think power is about winning the argument. But deeper power lies in defining what the argument is about.

What counts as urgent? What gets ignored? Which options are considered realistic? Which trade-offs are treated as acceptable? Which language is used to describe the problem? Which metrics decide success?

Whoever frames the question often controls the answer.

This is why rule-breaking can be powerful. Existing rules often protect incumbents. They define what is “normal”, “professional”, “serious”, “realistic” or “impossible”. Challenging the frame can open new space for action.

Agenda control is not just talking louder. It is deciding what enters the decision space.

The core question is:

> Can I define the rules, frame and decision space before others debate the outcome?

At its deepest level, power is not force. It is reality-framing.

# 5. Influence and exchange

The fifth axis is influence and exchange.

This is the familiar territory of persuasion, reciprocity, consultation, liking, trust, admiration, obligation and emotional alignment.

People rarely move because of formal authority alone. They move because they feel seen, respected, helped, inspired, protected, included, indebted or convinced.

This is why soft influence often beats pressure. Pressure can produce compliance, but it also produces resistance, resentment and hidden sabotage. Consultation, collaboration and rational persuasion can create ownership.

Reciprocity also matters. Help given at the right moment creates remembered value. But there is a difference between strategic generosity and invisible labour. Power-building generosity is legible, bounded and connected to durable relationships.

The core question is:

> Can I move people without relying on brute force?

Durable power works through consent, trust, identification, obligation and perceived competence.

# 6. Power deployment

The sixth axis is power deployment.

Power that is never used decays.

Many people accumulate social capital, goodwill, expertise or authority, but then hesitate to spend it. They avoid asking for resources. They avoid making decisions. They avoid rewarding allies. They avoid removing obstacles. They avoid taking visible responsibility.

But unused power is not neutral. It signals uncertainty. It allows others to fill the vacuum. It teaches people that your influence is symbolic rather than operational.

Deployment means using power to create outcomes.

It means asking. Deciding. Allocating. Protecting. Rewarding. Building. Enforcing boundaries. Taking the shot.

This is also where visible success compounds. Once results appear, people reinterpret the path that produced them. Tactics that looked ambitious may later look visionary. Risks that looked excessive may later look obvious. Confidence that looked premature may later look justified.

The core question is:

> Do I actually use power to produce visible outcomes?

Power compounds when it becomes evidence.

# 7. Organisational and system capacity

The seventh axis is often neglected because it is not glamorous.

It is organisational capacity.

Administration. Systems. Processes. Files. Calendars. Dashboards. Documentation. Legal structures. Workflows. Delegation. Finance. Governance. Institutional memory.

This kind of “boring power” is often what separates temporary influence from durable power.

A charismatic person can attract attention. A politically skilled person can win allies. A persuasive person can create momentum. But without organisational capacity, the system collapses back into personal effort.

You forget. You miss follow-ups. You lose track of promises. You duplicate work. You depend on one person’s memory. You cannot scale. You cannot hand over. You cannot survive stress.

Organisational capacity converts influence into machinery.

It creates repeatable routines. It preserves knowledge beyond one conversation. It reduces single points of failure. It allows a group to keep functioning when energy, mood or personnel change.

The core question is:

> Can I turn influence into repeatable machinery?

This is the administrative substrate of power.

# 8. Opportunity and breakthrough capacity

The eighth axis is opportunity and breakthrough capacity.

This is the ability to detect and act on rare, high-upside openings.

Not all risks are equal. Some are reckless. Some are necessary. Some are asymmetric: the downside is limited, but the upside could change the game.

Breakthrough power comes from recognising these moments before consensus forms.

A new technology appears. A market shifts. A gatekeeper loses control. A platform opens distribution. A rival hesitates. A cultural narrative changes. A neglected niche becomes legible. A new coalition becomes possible.

Most people wait for certainty. But by the time certainty arrives, the opportunity has often been absorbed by faster actors.

This does not mean acting randomly. It means preserving optionality, maintaining enough slack to move, and being willing to act before the crowd agrees.

The core question is:

> Can I detect and act on rare, high-upside openings before they close?

In exponential environments, early movement matters because small advantages can compound.

# The IQ/EQ kernel

The eight axes describe the structure of power.

But underneath them sits something deeper: the IQ/EQ kernel.

IQ and EQ are not simply two more rules of power. They are the cognitive-emotional operating system that determines whether the rules can be selected, sequenced and executed well.

IQ, in this context, does not just mean test performance. It means cognitive capacity for modelling systems, abstracting patterns, tracking causal relations, calculating risks, learning quickly and planning over time.

It supports questions like:

- What is really causing the outcome?
- Which lever matters most?
- Where is the hidden dependency?
- What happens if the system changes?
- Which opportunity is asymmetric rather than merely exciting?

EQ, in this context, does not just mean being nice. It means social-emotional capacity: reading people, regulating yourself under pressure, sensing status dynamics, building trust, repairing conflict, detecting resentment, and framing ideas in ways that resonate with identity and emotion.

It supports questions like:

- What does this person actually need?
- What fear is shaping their resistance?
- Who feels threatened?
- Which alliance is fragile?
- When should I push, pause, defer, flatter, challenge or withdraw?

High IQ without EQ can become brittle. It may produce clever strategy that fails because it misreads people.

High EQ without IQ can become socially fluent but strategically weak. It may build warmth and trust without accurately modelling systems, incentives, timing or risk.

The strongest form is integrated:

> High IQ + high EQ + organisational capacity.

That combination can read systems, read people, build machinery and act when opportunity appears.

# Power as adaptive capacity, not domination

The older model of power says:

> Get influence.

The fuller model says:

> Build the capacity to shape reality without losing feedback.

That difference is crucial.

Influence alone can be shallow. Status can be brittle. Charisma can fade. Networks can decay. Resources can be captured. Authority can become self-protective. Even legitimacy can become hollow if it is no longer coupled to reality.

Adaptive power is different. It expands the system’s 𝒢-volume: the range of viable roles, futures, experiments, relationships, narratives and coordinated actions that remain open to the person, group or institution.

## Figure 3. Healthy power vs pathological power

| Healthy power expands | Pathological power narrows |
|---|---|
| More viable roles | Captured attention |
| More reachable futures | Rigid hierarchy |
| More adaptive experiments | Fear-based compliance |
| Better public models | False public models |
| Better feedback | Suppressed feedback |
| More flexible norms | Brittle legitimacy |

This is why power cannot be understood only as domination.

Domination may produce compliance, but it often reduces intelligence. It suppresses feedback, distorts public models, narrows role flexibility, and makes the system less capable of learning.

Adaptive power does the opposite. It increases the capacity of the system to explore, constrain, coordinate and consolidate.

It can build institutions, protect autonomy, fund alternatives, resist capture, organise people, create options, recover from stress, and make humane projects real.

In a world increasingly shaped by AI, platforms, information operations, finance, network effects and geopolitical competition, power is becoming more model-based. Whoever shapes the public model shapes what people attend to. Whoever shapes the agenda shapes what is thinkable. Whoever controls the infrastructure shapes what is possible. Whoever controls the proof standard shapes what is legitimate.

This is why the eight axes matter.

1. Self-positioning shapes whether others can imagine you as a consequential actor.
2. Resource control shapes dependency and bargaining power.
3. Networks shape information, sponsorship and opportunity flow.
4. Agenda and framing control shapes what enters the decision space.
5. Influence and exchange shape consent, trust and alignment.
6. Power deployment turns stored capacity into outcomes.
7. Organisational capacity converts influence into machinery.
8. Opportunity capacity allows early movement when the payoff curve steepens.

Underneath all eight sits the IQ/EQ kernel.

IQ models systems, risks, causal structure and opportunity.

EQ reads people, regulates emotion, preserves trust and keeps feedback open.

Together, they make power intelligent rather than merely forceful.
