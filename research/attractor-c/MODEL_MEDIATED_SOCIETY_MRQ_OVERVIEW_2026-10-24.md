# The Model-Mediated Society: Western Political Economy, Hyperreality and Attractor C

**Kastel Futures / Multipolar Red Queen (MRQ) research overview**  
**Version:** 0.1 — 24 October 2026  
**Status:** Analytical synthesis and research hypotheses; not a verified forecast or canonical revision of MRQ  
**Scope:** Western liberal democracies as the primary empirical setting, with conditional implications for other political economies  
**Repository role:** Power-politics research. This document does not amend Kastel Stack software specifications or Kastel World fictional canon.

## Executive thesis

A potentially important change in modern political-economic-cultural life is the growing integration of three systems that were once treated more separately: (1) state security and industrial strategy, (2) civilian platform-mediated markets and organisational management, and (3) the digitally mediated production of information, social identity and experience. Advances in AI, data infrastructure, automation and operational modelling allow these systems to observe, classify, coordinate and act on more of everyday life.

The strongest version of the Kastel Futures hypothesis is **not** that Western states have installed one centrally managed Matrix. It is that competition among governments and firms can favour interoperable systems of legibility, prediction, optimisation and action. As people participate in these systems to work, consume, acquire recognition and seek enjoyment, some of the systems' operating categories may become internalised as ordinary understandings of success, value, possibility and selfhood.

The pivotal transition is from a **model of society** to a society increasingly **organised through models**—and, ultimately, individuals partially **modelling themselves through the categories and feedback of those models**. This possibility links operational digital twins to the sociology of performative measures, the endogenous formation of preferences, mediated social identity and Baudrillard's philosophical account of hyperreality.

This overview distinguishes observed developments from mechanisms with supporting research and the unproven hypothesis of **Attractor C**, a self-reinforcing institutional–behavioural–cognitive regime. Political disagreement may remain intense within such a regime: common infrastructural dependencies need not produce one shared explicit ideology. Likewise, compelling digital entertainment can supply genuine wellbeing and opportunities; its possible stabilising effect under material deprivation should not be confused with proof of deliberate state pacification.

### Epistemic labels

- **O — Observable/documented:** Public policy, official technical architecture, established product behaviour, or directly observed research outcomes within stated populations and dates.
- **M — Mechanism supported:** A social, psychological or economic causal mechanism with substantive theory and/or evidence; may not generalise across contexts.
- **H — Hypothesis/extrapolation:** The proposed synthesis, societal-scale feedback or future pathway has not been adequately tested.
- **N — Normative/design:** A preferred principle or proposed intervention rather than an empirical observation.

The terms *Blue Rail*, *Red Rail*, *Attractors A/B/C*, *objective-function monism*, *compensatory hyperreality* and *Recursive Identity Alignment* are Kastel conceptual vocabulary, not universally accepted academic categories.

## 2. From MRQ power competition to integrated socio-technical infrastructure

The supplied MRQ paper argues that modern power cannot be understood only through comparatively traditional assets such as military mass, territory, energy, finance or authority. AI-based cognitive capital, network infrastructure, information operations and digital finance may exhibit compounding and network-dependent advantages. In a multipolar Red Queen environment, rival actors have incentives to keep investing simply to preserve their relative standing.

The paper's alignment parameter **phi** concerns the assimilation or alignment of subordinate actors into a larger power trajectory; **delta** concerns sensitivity to differences in power. They belong to the author's model and are not empirically estimated coefficients for contemporary states in this overview. A transition from state-level alignment to cross-sector digital integration is therefore an *extension* of MRQ, not something the original equations have demonstrated.

The potential causal sequence is:

> Geopolitical competition → incentives for strategic technological investment → digitally integrated industrial and security capabilities → reduced coordination costs and greater organisational legibility → further competitive advantages and pressure to imitate.

Crucially, **alignment need not mean direct ownership**. Standards, procurement dependence, compatible software, financing, workforce pipelines and access to markets can align nominally independent entities while leaving their corporate and legal identities intact.

### Evidence of an industrial-security turn

**O. United Kingdom.** The 2025 UK National Security Strategy explicitly proposes broadening the defence industrial base to encompass academia, dual-use civilian–military firms, financial services, technologists and trade unions. The Defence Industrial Strategy of September 2025 frames defence as an engine of growth, stresses procurement reform, and seeks spillovers into the wider economy. This is documentary evidence for more extensive strategic industrial integration; it does **not** itself prove domestic mass behavioural control. Sources: [UK National Security Strategy 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-security-strategy-2025-security-for-the-british-people-in-a-dangerous-world/national-security-strategy-2025-security-for-the-british-people-in-a-dangerous-world-html); [Defence Industrial Strategy 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/defence-industrial-strategy-2025-making-defence-an-engine-for-growth).

**O. European Union.** EU Readiness 2030 / ReArm Europe includes a proposed mobilisation of up to €800 billion through a mixture of national fiscal flexibility, an EU loan instrument and other financing, with joint procurement and defence-industrial strengthening. This is a plan and potential financing envelope, not proof that €800 billion has been spent. Source: [European Commission: Future of European defence](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/defence/future-european-defence_en).

**M/H. Security-industrial convergence.** The supplied *Rogue by Design?* piece argues that multi-domain operations blur the peace–war divide by institutionalising coordination across physical, cyber and informational domains. It should be treated as a critical interpretation, not as an automatic inference that every civilian technology company is directed by a security establishment. Alternative explanations include deterrence, supply-chain resilience, civil emergency preparedness and economic efficiency.

### Isembard as an illustrative, not universal, organisational type

A network of distributed production units using shared operational software and serving dual-use strategic industries illustrates a paradox central to this argument: **physical distribution can coexist with concentrated digital coordination**. Isembard's software-coordinated advanced-manufacturing model provides a relevant example. The broad pattern matters more than claims about one firm's growth, contracts or political intent. It would be a mistake to infer from this firm alone that all Western states pursue identical industrial institutions.

## 3. Attractors A, B and C: states of organisation, not settled historical stages

The conversational model distinguishes three useful ideal types:

| Attractor | Principal features | Relevant research question |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Distributed competition** | Many relatively independent firms, institutions, information and decision systems | Do interoperability and competition preserve independent experimentation and realistic exit? |
| **B — Concentrated AI infrastructure** | Network effects, compounding data/compute advantages, platform dependence, large operational control surfaces | Who controls critical interfaces, standards, data rights and the action layer? |
| **C — Institutional–behavioural–cognitive convergence and hyperreal mediation** | Widely used digital intermediaries affect incentives, routinised behaviour, cognitive assumptions, identity and perceived possibilities | When, for whom and under what conditions do platform models shape self-understanding and reproduce institutional dependence? |

They are **candidate attractors**, not demonstrated dynamical basins. Demonstrating a real attractor would require identifiable state variables, reinforcing feedback, threshold effects, persistence after perturbation and empirical differentiation from plausible alternative models.

Attractor C does not logically follow from B in all circumstances. Open protocols, competition, regulation, civic institutions, local capacity and user exit can interrupt or reverse concentration and internalisation. The supplied *Assess MRQ Evidence* note explicitly examines counter-concentration through Holling's adaptive cycle, ecological analogies and polycentric governance; those analogies are heuristics, not proof that a digital economy must decentralise.

## 4. The infrastructural interface: consumer life meets strategic industry

Consumer and security-related industries increasingly depend on overlapping inputs: semiconductors, energy systems, communications, cloud computing, software, robotics, sensors and AI models. This creates channels through which state-backed industrial priorities and ordinary private markets can reinforce one another.

The extension into everyday life need not arrive as a conspicuously militarised consumer interface. It can appear as cheaper services, domestic employment, advanced devices, personalised assistants, frictionless payments, more reliable logistics or digital safety. The people using such products usually have their own purposes and can benefit genuinely from them.

A useful distinction:

1. **Technological overlap (O):** Civilian and strategic sectors use related technologies and suppliers.
2. **Economic incentive alignment (M):** Procurement, jobs and investment create incentives for business participation in priority sectors.
3. **Institutional integration (M/H):** Common standards and digital tools may increase interoperability and shared dependency.
4. **Psychological alignment (H at scale):** Repeated participation may shape what people consider normal, desirable and possible.
5. **Deliberate population control (unsupported as a general inference):** This would require evidence of specific actors, coordination, operational programmes and effects.

The coexistence of these layers must not be collapsed into the assertion that states and firms share a single comprehensive objective function. Their incentives overlap in some domains and conflict in others.

## 5. Digital twins and ontologies: from map to operating surface

Palantir describes its Ontology as an operational layer connecting enterprise data and models to real-world counterparts. It explicitly includes **semantic elements** (objects, properties, links) and **kinetic elements** (actions, functions and security). Palantir's architectural description connects **data, logic, action and security**, making representation actionable. Sources: [Palantir Ontology overview](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/ontology); [Palantir Ontology system](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/architecture-center/ontology-system).

**O:** Such systems can coordinate work through digital models with defined actions. **M:** Models can become performative when the actors being measured change their behaviour in response to the representations. **H:** Sufficiently pervasive model-mediated institutions might transform people's wider cognitive assumptions about the social world.

The causal loop is:

> Activities and people → selected machine-legible categories → predictions, rankings and decision rules → resource allocation and action → adaptive behaviours → modified categories and conditions.

This is where the comparison to Baudrillard's *Simulacra and Simulation* becomes analytically valuable. The **map can participate in producing the territory**. But an industrial ontology is *not* literally a simulation without referents: physical inventory, staff, patients, orders and facilities still exist. The strongest operational connection to Baudrillard is the growing authority of models, not proof that material reality has vanished.

MacKenzie's *An Engine, Not a Camera* documents performativity in finance; Espeland and Sauder show how rankings can change institutions; Ian Hacking examines looping effects of classifications applied to people; James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* explores the limits and consequences of administrative simplification.

A model's **ontological omissions** become serious when the organisation acts as though the model includes everything worth valuing. Goodhart-type incentives may reward what is easiest to measure. However, transparent, revisable and independently evaluated ontologies can also strengthen accountability and reveal previously neglected realities.

## 6. Behavioural alignment is a pathway to cognitive alignment

The conversation developed a central correction: **operationally similar behaviour and internally held beliefs are not wholly independent**.

- **Bourdieu — habitus/doxa:** Social structures can become embodied in durable dispositions and taken-for-granted practical assumptions.
- **Berger and Luckmann — social construction:** Habitual practices become institutions and are internalised as ordinary social reality.
- **Samuel Bowles — endogenous preferences:** Economic institutions can shape tastes, the framing of choices, psychological dispositions and values, rather than merely satisfying fixed preferences. See [Bowles (1998)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/jeclit/v36y1998i1p75-111.html).
- **Gramsci — hegemony/common sense:** Dominant institutions partly reproduce themselves by shaping what counts as natural and reasonable.
- **Foucault and Wendy Brown — governmentality and market rationality:** Governance and market logics can enter how people understand themselves as workers, consumers and economic actors.

These are different theoretical traditions. None alone proves uniform psychological convergence across AI users.

A plausible process is:

> Repeated participation under common incentives → behavioural adjustment → habit, expectation and preference learning → partly internalised decision schemas → renewed participation and institutional reproduction.

For example, individuals repeatedly evaluated through employability scores, professional rankings and algorithmic hiring may come to treat the metrics as natural descriptions of personal merit. Such internalisation depends on the value attached to the metric, available alternatives, social context and personal reflection.

The **OECD's December 2025 six-country employer survey** provides direct evidence that algorithmic management is already prevalent in the workplaces surveyed: at least one instruction, monitoring or evaluation tool was reported by managers at 90% of surveyed US firms and, on average, 79% of those in France, Germany, Italy and Spain; the comparable figure in Japan was 40%. These are *surveyed managers' reports*, not a census of all enterprises or evidence of common subjective beliefs. Source: [OECD: How widespread is algorithmic management in workplaces?](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/how-widespread-is-algorithmic-management-in-workplaces_cda7a114-en/full-report.html).

## 7. A common structural core with plural political identities

A distinctive implication is that people can become more **institutionally and behaviourally similar** without becoming more **ideologically similar**.

Two groups may differ sharply in religious, social or political beliefs but still rely on the same platforms for income, communications, commerce, AI assistance, credentials and search. The shared infrastructure can impose common forms of visibility, ranking, market access and performance measurement even as the content consumed within it diverges.

Nancy Fraser's redistribution/recognition distinction offers a historical political-economic precedent for cultural conflict coexisting with overlapping economic assumptions. Mark Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* asks how alternatives can become difficult to envisage within a dominant social order; Gramsci provides a theory of shared practical common sense.

But **culture-war conflict cannot be presumed fake, superficial or centrally scripted**. Debates over legal rights, welfare, immigration, religion or gender can have materially different consequences. The falsifiable proposition is narrower: *some explicit ideological polarisation may coexist with convergence in underlying institutional dependence and practical decision criteria*.

Digital platforms can also monetise political disagreement. Whether that strengthens the broader institutional system, produces fragmentation, or enables mobilisation depends on platform design and context.

## 8. Hyperreality: not just simulated worlds, but model-mediated meaning

Baudrillard's **hyperreality** concerns a condition in which signs, models and representations help constitute what is experienced as real. It is more radical than the simple observation that people spend time online. Debord's *Society of the Spectacle* foregrounds social relations mediated through representations; Postman's *Amusing Ourselves to Death* develops an account of entertainment's effects on public discourse.

Three analytically separable forms are proposed:

| Form | What is mediated | Illustrative arena |
|---|---|---|
| **Institutional** | Organisational state, decisions and eligibility | Enterprise ontologies, recruitment systems, credit and service access |
| **Experiential** | Pleasure, participation, competence, belonging and achievement | Social media, gaming, virtual environments, personalised media |
| **Subjective** | The individual's identity, worth, prospects and aspiration | Self-branding, engagement metrics, model-mediated self-evaluation |

The third is the decisive extension. Repeated experience of the platform's reward structure may not merely change what people do; it may shift **what they believe they are**, **what success means**, and **which futures they consider worth pursuing**.

No claim is made that these representations are always false or inferior. Online friendships, achievements and income may be genuine. The critical variable is whether model-mediated rewards **expand** the person's actionable possibilities or **crowd out** other opportunities and independent criteria of value.

## 9. Recursive Identity Alignment and ontological narrowing

**Recursive Identity Alignment (H):** The proposed process in which individuals repeatedly adapt self-presentation, behaviour and aspirations to digitally mediated categories of recognition, and may subsequently internalise those categories into self-concept and perceived opportunity.

**Ontological narrowing (H):** A relative reduction in the range of categories through which a person evaluates worthwhile achievements, identities or futures, particularly when platform measures become dominant and alternative values become less salient.

Illustrative feedback:

> Person and multidimensional life → partial digital representation → ranking, reach and reward → adjustment of presentation and conduct → learned expectations about self-worth → further orientation towards the same representation.

Consider someone who receives high engagement for glamorous photographs in luxury hotels. Such images may become not just records of valued experiences but *objectives of the experiences themselves*. Beauty, affluence, desirability and freedom can become expressed as visibility, photogenic luxury, followers and aspirational imagery. The person may then seek situations primarily because they produce recognisable status symbols.

This is an **illustration**, not a general claim about women, attractive people or influencers. Platform-mediated self-modelling also occurs in professional status, athletic performance, finance, political commentary, masculinity and academic visibility.

Supporting literatures include Cooley's *looking-glass self*, Fredrickson and Roberts' objectification theory, Hacking's looping classifications and research on social comparison. A 2025 three-level meta-analysis of 68 papers (218 effect sizes) reported a positive association between social-media use and self-objectification (**r = 0.207**); this does not establish causality or universal identity capture. Sources: [Fredrickson & Roberts (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1471-6402.1997.tb00108.x); [2025 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40311165/).

The **critical-mass hypothesis** is a separate step: individual feedback may scale through imitation, commercial supply, prestige hierarchies and platform distribution, making particular models of success appear natural to wider populations. Cultural effects need not require a numerical majority of users to be deeply internalised. Neither the existence of such a threshold nor its attainment has been established.

## 10. Compensatory hyperreality and constrained material opportunity

The supplied conversation proposes **compensatory hyperreality (H)**: increasingly engaging digitally mediated experiences may provide accessible competence, autonomy, recognition, belonging, adventure or perceived possibility when offline opportunities are constrained.

The contrast is not simply "real" versus "fake". It is between **materially achievable options** and **psychologically rewarding represented or digitally enacted options**.

Self-determination theory identifies competence, autonomy and relatedness as important motivational needs. Kardefelt-Winther's compensatory internet-use framework suggests that some excessive use can reflect attempts to cope with psychosocial problems rather than simple technological addiction. Source: [Kardefelt-Winther (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2013.10.059).

One econometric study found that improvements in recreational computing increased the attractiveness of leisure and contributed to changes in market work hours among US men aged 21–30 in the period studied; it did not establish that gaming causes poverty or serves a government pacification strategy. Source: [Aguiar, Bils, Charles & Hurst (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1086/711916).

The proposed sequence is:

> Material or social need frustration → greater attractiveness of digital compensation for some people → engagement and learning of digital rewards → possible displacement **or enhancement** of offline activity → changed relative attractiveness of opportunities.

Two opposite outcomes must remain in the model. Digital participation can **substitute for** difficult offline opportunities, but it can also **build** skills, income, relationships, collective organisation and practical agency.

### "Population management": distinguish three meanings

**O/M. Commercial engagement management:** Firms routinely optimise discovery, retention, conversion and consumer experience.

**H. Emergent social stabilisation:** Attractive digital rewards may reduce the felt urgency of some unmet material needs or displace some forms of mobilisation, even without central coordination.

**Specific allegation requiring direct evidence. Deliberate political pacification:** A government or coordinated set of firms intentionally substitutes immersive consumption for material opportunity to reduce opposition. This cannot be inferred from engagement design, inequality, entertainment popularity or defence-industrial policy alone.

The hypotheses may have different implications across social groups and countries; no universal effect is assumed.

## 11. The consumer economy as interface, not a separate regime

The general consumer economy supplies a possible route by which structural integration can remain experientially familiar. People seek useful products, jobs, entertainment and status. Platforms seek revenue and engagement. States seek security, prosperity and administratively usable capabilities. These incentives can coincide without becoming identical.

An everyday AI assistant illustrates the issue. It may reduce the cost of searching, comparing, remembering and transacting, but the system that chooses the default menu of alternatives can also become a consequential intermediary. The strategic question is **who controls the discovery and action interface**, and whether users can check the grounds of recommendations and genuinely exit.

Attractor B becomes relevant when cloud, compute, identity, payments, distribution or model services are concentrated. Attractor C becomes relevant when the presentation of opportunities and the behavioural standards required to obtain them begin influencing individual evaluation and self-understanding.

This is not an argument that technological convenience is a disguised military operation. It is a hypothesis about cumulative institutional dependencies and habit formation in dual-use and civilian economies.

## 12. Evidence of algorithmic influence: concrete but bounded

Three findings constrain the stronger theoretical interpretation.

**O. Political attitudes and following behaviour.** A 2026 *Nature* field experiment randomly assigned active US-based X users to algorithmic and chronological feeds for seven weeks. Turning the algorithmic feed on affected certain political attitudes and following behaviour. Switching it off did not symmetrically reverse those effects. The study found no significant effects on self-reported partisanship or affective polarisation. This is platform-, population- and period-specific, not proof of universal convergence. [Gauthier et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10098-2).

**O. Perceptions of social norms.** A 2026 *Nature* study randomly assigned 2,000 participants to different feed-ranking algorithms for eight weeks around the 2024 US election; ranking choices affected exposure and perceptions of partisan norms, and an alternative ranking design improved some outcomes without reducing enjoyment. This shows that design is consequential and that less distorting alternatives can exist. [Brady et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10536-1).

**O/M. Self-objectification and social media.** The positive meta-analytic association noted in Section 9 supports a mechanism involving some users' self-evaluation, but cannot establish a continuous identity-feedback loop, a population threshold or the direction of causality on its own.

Important limiting evidence includes earlier platform experiments finding little or no effect on some political outcomes, heterogeneous effects of digital participation and real benefits from online social connection and enjoyment.

## 13. Causal architecture: coupled loops, not a single controller

The synthesised model has seven interacting loops.

| Loop | Main actors | Reinforcing mechanism | Counterforce or failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R1 — MRQ competition** | States, alliances, strategic firms | Relative threat → investment in leverage and integration → pressure to keep pace | Diplomacy, arms control, fiscal limits, strategic de-escalation |
| **R2 — Platform concentration** | Major technology intermediaries | Scale/data/distribution → better services or lock-in → further demand | Competition, open standards, portability, regulation |
| **R3 — Operational ontology** | Firms, public agencies, vendors | Model → decision → changed reality → reliance on model | Independent outcome audit, contestability, human judgement |
| **R4 — Behaviour–cognition** | Workers, consumers, institutions | Rewarded conduct → habits and preferences → institutional legitimacy | Reflection, contrary experiences, plural institutions |
| **R5 — Recursive identity** | Users, audiences, creators, platforms | Ranking and recognition → self-presentation → self-concept → further engagement | Alternative status cultures, offline roles and identities |
| **R6 — Compensatory hyperreality** | Households, entertainment platforms, communities | Unmet needs → rewarding mediated experiences → engagement | Meaningful offline opportunities; constructive digital uses |
| **R7 — Cultural contestation** | Political groups, media, platforms | Polarising content → attention and mobilisation → more mediated participation | Civic deliberation, offline organisation, heterogeneous platforms |

**Coupling** is the actual research question. R1–R7 need not all be present, and one loop may inhibit another. Attractor C would require demonstrable reinforcement and persistence across several loops.

A particularly important asymmetry is that **political belief polarisation can coexist with convergent dependence on infrastructure and decision procedures**. Divergent narratives may be delivered over common commercial rails.

The original MRQ mathematical model does not yet estimate these added feedbacks or establish that Attractor C is a dynamical attractor. Any new computational model must keep the causal extension distinguishable from the original.

## 14. West, other blocs, and the limit of global extrapolation

The UK's and EU's strategic-industrial priorities and the US-/Europe-focused social-platform experiments make Western economies a justified starting point. But the *global* proposition requires comparison.

- **United States:** Investigate large-platform market structure, private-sector AI investment, federal procurement and the comparatively high prevalence of reported algorithmic management.
- **European Union / UK:** Investigate differing forms of industrial coordination, defence integration, market regulation, labour protections and consumer rights. Do not assume that UK policy represents all EU member states.
- **China:** A potentially contrasting configuration involving large commercial platforms and more direct political and regulatory involvement in parts of the digital economy. Claims about unified national scoring or comprehensive behavioural control require programme-specific evidence; they must not be assumed.
- **India and other populous emerging economies:** Examine public digital infrastructure, identity and payments, open interfaces, private-platform intermediation and differences in access and governance.
- **Gulf states:** Examine sovereign-led digital infrastructure, public investment, smart-city systems and administrative integration, distinguishing ambition from actual implementation.
- **Global South more broadly:** Platform import dependence, informal economies, mobile-money innovation, uneven digital access and local institutional diversity may produce mixed patterns rather than straightforward convergence.

These are comparative *research programmes*, not empirical findings about all countries. Societies with strong informal networks, local production, alternative institutions and incomplete digitisation may resist some pathways while facing other dependencies.

## 15. Alternative explanations and counter-scenarios

A rigorous model should consider at least five rivals.

1. **Efficiency without capture:** Operational models may improve coordination and individual options without reducing independent judgement.
2. **Institutional pluralism:** Competing platforms, regulations and civil associations may maintain genuine choice and independent understandings.
3. **Fragmentation rather than convergence:** Shared architecture may host increasingly incompatible identities and local worlds without common cognitive schemas.
4. **Material constraint without pacification:** Insecurity may intensify protest, organising and rejection of digital intermediaries rather than stimulate compliant immersion.
5. **Productive hyperreality:** Gaming, online communities and AI tools may increase skill, resilience, cultural exchange and income, enlarging rather than narrowing opportunity.

Counter-concentration is not merely a hope: the Kastel source corpus explicitly places polycentric organisation, decentralised infrastructures and adaptive recovery within its MRQ analysis. Their viability remains conditional on cost, collective governance, coordination and competitive pressures.

## 16. Predictions and measurement programme

A claim that a "critical mass" has adopted model-mediated identities should be treated as a **threshold hypothesis to estimate**, not a present-day fact.

| Hypothesis | Leading indicators | Better test / challenge |
|---|---|---|
| **H1: Shared platforms precede behavioural convergence** | Common ranking criteria; similar response patterns; switching costs | Longitudinal comparative cohorts with differential platform exposure |
| **H2: Behavioural adaptation precedes cognitive internalisation** | Temporal change in aspirations, decision criteria and perceived necessity | Repeated measurement; quasi-exogenous access changes; control for selection |
| **H3: Model authority reshapes institutional reality** | KPI gaming, resource reallocation, shifts in omitted outcomes | Staggered ontology rollout, independent outcomes, pre/post and matched comparison |
| **H4: Recursive identity alignment narrows possibility spaces** | Greater weight placed on platform status; fewer unaided alternative goals | Randomised ranking-feedback interventions; independent self-concept assessments |
| **H5: Material constraint moderates compensatory digital demand** | Opportunity, need frustration, engagement motives, online/offline outcomes | Longitudinal cross-lagged and natural-experiment designs |
| **H6: Cultural polarisation coexists with shared practical schemas** | Ideological distance versus similarity in economic decision criteria | Multi-group measurement invariance; cross-country replication |
| **H7: Institutional concentration can prompt counter-concentration** | Adoption of interoperable alternatives, local institutions, portability | Comparative policy shocks; survival/viability of alternative networks |

### Measurement contract

Prefer *outcomes outside the system being optimised*. A platform's engagement score cannot independently prove flourishing; an employer's productivity rating cannot independently prove employee wellbeing; a social-media popularity measure cannot independently establish the quality of relationships.

Candidate outcome domains:

- **Material optionality:** Housing and occupational accessibility, discretionary resources, switching costs, access to independent infrastructure.
- **Behavioural dependence:** Percentage of economically consequential tasks mediated by specific intermediaries and successful ability to exit.
- **Cognitive schemas:** Unaided option generation, causal explanations of success, value prioritisation, trust calibration and alternative consideration.
- **Identity:** Self-concept diversity, salience of platform ratings, discrepancy between preferred and rewarded self-presentation.
- **Experience:** Psychological need satisfaction online/offline, motivations for use, wellbeing and displacement versus complementarity.
- **Institutional health:** Contestability, rights of appeal, data portability, human oversight, independent audit and robustness to shocks.
- **Geopolitical coupling:** Procurement and industrial concentration, cross-sector dependencies, interoperability and civilian–defence technology overlap.

Stratify by socioeconomic circumstances, age, geography, occupational position and digital access; avoid treating an elite or highly connected sample as a population census.

### Minimal causal graph

MRQ rivalry → strategic investment → infrastructural integration → ontology-mediated work/markets → repeated behavioural adaptation → changes in practical schemas → institutional reproduction.

A separate moderated pathway runs from **material opportunity constraints** to **compensatory mediated engagement**, potentially affecting offline agency and institutional participation.

A third loop runs from **platform visibility and rewards** to **identity performance**, **self-understanding**, and **future platform participation**.

Do not infer any downstream arrow merely because an upstream arrow has been observed.

## 17. Power, agency and the two rails

The *Axes of Power* document defines eight interacting capacities: self-positioning, resource control, network architecture, agenda/framing, influence/exchange, deployment, organisational capacity and opportunity/breakthrough. This provides a cross-scale diagnostic for who controls model construction, incentives, action rights and exit opportunities.

**Healthy power (N)** expands meaningful roles, accurate feedback, causal criticism, practical alternatives and adaptive recovery. **Pathological power (N)** narrows alternatives, captures attention, suppresses error correction and turns public models into self-protecting fictions. These are explicit Kastel design values, not neutral measurements.

**Blue Rail** denotes effective participation in mainstream institutional, technological, distribution and market infrastructures. It can legitimately generate income, efficiency and opportunity. **Red Rail** denotes deliberate investment in portable knowledge, independent capacity, human-scale institutions, privacy, pluralism and the ability to refuse or exit unsuitable dependencies. The supplied *Matrix* essay proposes using reversible Blue Rail gains to help fund Red Rail options.

The supplied **NAPA Charter** grounds that strategy in nonviolence, lawful and legible institutions, human dignity, scientific and artistic freedom, distributed governance and open commons. This paper does not imply that civil withdrawal is invariably superior to democratic reform or public accountability. A plural response may combine consumer protection, antitrust, labour voice, democratic governance, accessible technology, local provision and portable privately governed alternatives.

### Implications for AI and adaptive intelligence design

A system using an ontology to optimise operations should remain capable of detecting at least four distinct errors:

- **Parameter error:** Wrong estimates within an otherwise appropriate model.
- **Structural error:** Missing or wrongly connected causal variables.
- **Ontological inadequacy:** Wrong categories or omission of important human realities.
- **Objective inadequacy:** Accurate optimisation of an inappropriate proxy or goal.

For Kastel Stack / GLIK / SWI and individual training systems such as Synergy IQ, a useful aspiration is to protect **reality coupling and agency**, not simply maximise platform-compatible performance. It is one thing to train attention or reasoning; it is another to establish real-world transfer, resilience, independent judgement or greater freedom. The latter remain independent empirical questions.

## 18. Integrated conclusion

The emerging picture is of a world in which geopolitical rivalry encourages investment in compounding technologies and strategic industrial capacity; firms and governments increasingly coordinate through machine-legible representations; consumer and work platforms mediate discovery, eligibility, action and reward; and people may adapt their habits, practical assumptions and self-understandings to those environments.

The central philosophical possibility is **hyperreal mediation**. Representations and computational ontologies may cease to function only as descriptions of institutions and persons. Through their roles in allocating resources, ranking performance, organising opportunities and rewarding self-presentation, they can participate in constituting the reality people encounter.

The strongest proposed Attractor C is therefore **not merely centralised digital control**. It is the **co-evolution of institutional operating models, behavioural routines, cognitive schemas, mediated experience and self-understanding**. A person can disagree politically with other users, or even consciously distrust a platform, while nevertheless organising everyday life through similar infrastructures and practical categories.

This is a researchable possibility, not proof that most individuals already inhabit a closed ontology or that governments have constructed a unified social simulation. The critical questions are which feedbacks are operating, for whom, how strongly, with what reversibility, and whether the result expands or contracts material and cognitive agency.

**Concise thesis:** The most consequential transition would occur when the digital model no longer merely represents the world, or governs actions within it, but also becomes a principal framework through which individuals recognise themselves and imagine their futures.
